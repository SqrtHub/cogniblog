Grading an open-ended response gets harder when the criteria only take shape after a stack of assessments lands on your desk. What counts as sufficient reasoning? Does a correct but underdeveloped idea meet the question's objective? Should writing style be part of this assessment?

A rubric makes these choices explicit. It describes what you will look for and what distinguishes one performance level from another. It can guide the activity, support grading, and give students a reference for improving their responses.

This guide shows how to prepare a short rubric for an open-ended question. The example was created to explain the method. It does not reproduce a real assessment or a real student's response.

## Start with what the question should reveal

Write one sentence describing what you want to see in the response. Choose an action tied to learning: explaining a relationship, supporting a position, comparing ideas, or showing the reasoning behind a conclusion.

"Understand the topic" is too broad to guide grading. "Defend a proposal with a reason connected to the problem" gives you something more specific to look for.

Then read the question. Does it give the student a chance to demonstrate that learning? If you want to assess reasoning, the prompt needs to ask for an explanation, not just a selected answer.

## Choose criteria you can recognize in the response

Turn the objective into observable features. For an argument, these might be the proposal, the supporting reason, and the connection between them. The criteria will be different for another subject or activity.

Avoid relying on labels such as "good answer," "creativity," or "neatness." They do not tell you what to look for. If creativity is part of the objective, describe the evidence you expect: for example, a solution that differs from those already discussed and also meets the task's requirements.

Include writing style when it is part of the learning being assessed. If the main objective is to explain a phenomenon, do not add a stylistic requirement that the prompt and activity have not prepared students to meet.

A short list is often easier to apply. Each criterion should add something rather than penalize the same difficulty under several names.

## Describe the difference between levels

Choose levels that make sense for the task and explain what changes from one to the next. "Complete," "partial," and "not yet evident" can be a starting point, but labels alone are not enough.

For a "reasoning" criterion, for example, describe whether the response explains why the proposal could work, gives a relevant but underdeveloped reason, or offers no reason connected to the problem.

Descriptors need to distinguish situations you may encounter in student responses. If two levels say almost the same thing, choosing between them will be difficult.

## An example rubric for an open-ended question

**Illustrative prompt:** "Defend a proposal to reduce paper waste at school and explain why it could work."

**Objective:** present a proposal and support it with a reason connected to the problem.

The criteria and levels below are teaching choices for this example. Adapt them to your class, what students have studied, and the assessment rules for the activity.

### Criterion: proposal

- **Complete:** presents a clear action to reduce paper waste at school.
- **Partial:** expresses an intention connected to the problem, but the action is not yet clearly defined.
- **Not yet evident:** does not present a proposal connected to paper waste.

### Criterion: reasoning

- **Complete:** explains why the proposed action could reduce waste, with a clear connection between the action and its result.
- **Partial:** gives a reason connected to the topic but does not sufficiently explain how it supports the proposal.
- **Not yet evident:** gives no relevant reason or simply repeats the proposal.

### Criterion: connection between ideas

- **Complete:** connects the proposal to the supporting reason so the reader can follow the argument.
- **Partial:** includes relevant elements, but the relationship between them needs further development.
- **Not yet evident:** does not show an identifiable relationship between the proposal and its supporting reason.

Only include this connection as a separate criterion if it is part of the objective. If it repeats what you have already assessed under reasoning, you can combine the two. A rubric should help distinguish evidence, not multiply penalties for the same difficulty.

If the activity requires a grade, decide the weights and the relationship between levels and points before using the rubric. These depend on the importance of each learning objective and your assessment rules. There is no single conversion that fits every question.

## Check that the rubric fits the activity

Before applying the criteria to the full set of responses, try them with examples prepared for that purpose or discuss the descriptors with a colleague. Look for situations where the levels are hard to distinguish.

Check whether each criterion describes the learning the question asked students to demonstrate. Also consider whether it allows for a correct answer expressed differently from your example. A rubric does not have to restrict responses to one model sentence.

When more than one person is grading, discussing the descriptors and a few examples helps develop a shared interpretation. This supports consistency but does not remove every judgment call.

## Share expectations while students can still use them

Criteria are more useful when students can understand them before or during the activity, rather than only when they receive the result. Explain what each criterion means and, where useful, use a short example to show the difference between levels.

You do not need an extensive table for every task. For a short activity, a clear explanation of what you expect may be enough. The format depends on the students' age and the kind of work they are producing.

## Use the criteria to prepare feedback

A rubric helps identify what a response already demonstrates and what still needs development. In your feedback, connect that observation to an action the student can take.

In the example above, instead of writing only "develop this further," you might say:

> Your proposal is clear. The reasoning does not yet explain how the action would reduce paper use. Add that connection and give an example of how the proposal would work at school.

This feedback is also illustrative. It comments on the work and suggests a next step without attributing the difficulty to a personal characteristic of the student.

Provide an opportunity to use the feedback: completing the response, revising a section, or applying the same criterion in another activity. Feedback then has a use beyond being a note next to a grade.

## A planning checklist for your next question

You can record:

- **Objective:** what learning should the response demonstrate?
- **Prompt:** what does the question ask the student to do?
- **Criteria:** what evidence will you look for?
- **Levels:** what distinguishes a complete response from a partial one?
- **Scoring, if applicable:** how do the criteria relate to the grade?
- **Feedback:** what action could help the student improve?

Start with one question from your next assessment and adapt the checklist to what you need to observe. Organizing the criteria comes before choosing a tool to support grading. To learn about CogniScion's approach to handwritten assessments, [visit the website](https://cogniscion.com/).
